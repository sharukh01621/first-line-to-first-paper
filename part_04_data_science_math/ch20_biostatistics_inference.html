Using scipy.stats and statsmodels.api on Bangladesh maternal and child health survey data (mch_survey_sample), you will move from descriptive tables to publication-grade confidence intervals, effect sizes, risk/odds ratios, and adjusted epidemiological models.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Compute exact distribution probabilities with scipy.stats (Normal, Binomial) and verify the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) through reproducible NumPy Monte Carlo simulation.
  2. Estimate clinical proportions with 95% Wilson score confidence intervals, avoiding the zero-event collapse and boundary overshoot of naive Wald intervals.
  3. Choose and execute the right group-comparison test—Welch’s two-sample \(t\)-test with pooled Cohen’s \(d\), Mann-Whitney \(U\), One-Way ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis \(H\)—based on outcome scale, variance heterogeneity, and skewness.
  4. Audit \(2 \times 2\) contingency tables using Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) (correction=False), expected-cell checks (expected_min < 5), Fisher’s exact test, Risk Ratios (RR), and Odds Ratios (OR) with Woolf 95% confidence intervals.
  5. Guard multi-hypothesis analyses against false positives using Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjustment (scipy.stats.false_discovery_control).
  6. Clean messy epidemiological survey covariates and fit a multivariable logistic regression model in statsmodels.api.Logit, reporting exponentiated Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR), 95% CIs, and \(p\)-values for publication tables.

20.1 Stage 1 — First-Principles Intuition: From Descriptive Tables to Inferential Evidence

Across Chapters 16–19, you built the full computational foundation of modern data science: vectorized NumPy arrays, clean Pandas data pipelines, publication Matplotlib/Seaborn graphics, and linear algebra & calculus intuition. Yet a mean, median, or bar chart alone describes only the 500 households in your sample.

Peer reviewers, ministries of health, and clinical trialists ask a deeper question: Could this observed rural–urban anemia gap be mere sampling luck, how large is the effect clinically, and does it survive after controlling for confounders?

That leap—from finite sample to population claim—is statistical inference. Five core principles govern every test in this chapter:

  • Sampling Variability & the Central Limit Theorem: Even if individual antenatal care (ANC) visits are heavily right-skewed, the distribution of sample means \(\bar{X}\) across repeated samples approaches a Normal distribution centered at \(\mu\) with standard error \(\text{SE} = \sigma / \sqrt{n}\).
  • Interval Estimation Over Bare Point Estimates: Saying “anemia prevalence is 35.8%” hides sample-size precision. Reporting “35.8% (95% Wilson CI: 31.7%–40.1%)” communicates both estimate and uncertainty transparently.
  • Effect Size + \(p\)-Value Together: A \(p\)-value answers whether an observed difference is incompatible with the null hypothesis (\(H_0\)); it says nothing about clinical magnitude. Always pair \(p\)-values with effect sizes (Cohen’s \(d\), RR, OR, or aOR).
  • Multiple Testing Inflates False Positives: If you run 20 independent tests at \(\alpha = 0.05\) when every null is true, you expect one false positive by pure chance! Controlling the False Discovery Rate (FDR) via Benjamini-Hochberg prevents cherry-picking noise.
  • Crude vs. Adjusted Associations (Confounding): In mch_survey_sample, rural mothers show a slightly higher crude anemia rate than urban mothers—yet urban mothers also attend significantly more ANC visits. Multivariable logistic regression untangles which predictor actually drives the risk.
NoteMental Model — Two Questions Before Picking Any Statistical Test

Never memorize a wall of test names in isolation. Ask just two diagnostic questions:

  1. What is the outcome variable’s scale? Continuous/numeric (maternal_hb_g_dl), skewed count/ordinal (anc_visits), or binary/categorical (maternal_anemia)?
  2. Are you comparing 2 groups, \(k \ge 3\) groups, or adjusting for multiple covariates simultaneously?

Once you answer those two questions, the exact SciPy or Statsmodels tool follows deterministically!

20.2 Stage 2 — Visual Mental Model: Statistical Test Selector

Use this decision map whenever you move from exploratory data analysis (EDA) to formal hypothesis testing and multivariable modeling:

Notice how bivariate tests (Welch \(t\), Mann-Whitney \(U\), \(\chi^2\), ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis) populate Table 1 & Table 2 of a scientific paper, whereas multivariable regression (sm.Logit) powers Table 3, isolating independent Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR) while controlling for confounders.

WarningTwo Silent SciPy Defaults That Catch Researchers Off Guard
  1. scipy.stats.ttest_ind(a, b) defaults to equal_var=True (Student’s \(t\)-test). Always pass equal_var=False for Welch’s \(t\)-test, which stays valid when group variances or sample sizes differ.
  2. scipy.stats.chi2_contingency(table) defaults to correction=True (Yates’ continuity correction for \(2 \times 2\) tables), which is overly conservative. Pass correction=False for standard Pearson’s \(\chi^2\), and switch to stats.fisher_exact whenever expected_min < 5.

20.3.2 Probability Distributions & CLT Monte Carlo Simulation

# 1. Theoretical Normal & Binomial probabilities
p_anemic_hb = stats.norm.cdf(11.0, loc=11.3, scale=1.4)
p_20_or_more = stats.binom.sf(19, n=50, p=0.35)
print(f"P(Hb < 11.0 g/dL | N(11.3, 1.4)): {p_anemic_hb:.4f}")
print(f"P(X >= 20 cases | Binom(50, 0.35)): {p_20_or_more:.4f}")

# 2. Central Limit Theorem (CLT) from skewed Exponential(scale=2.0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
draws = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=(2000, 30))
sample_means = draws.mean(axis=1)
theo_se = 2.0 / math.sqrt(30)
print(f"CLT mean of 2,000 means: {sample_means.mean():.4f} (true=2.0)")
print(f"Empirical SE: {sample_means.std(ddof=1):.4f} "
      f"| Theoretical SE: {theo_se:.4f}")

Expected Output:

P(Hb < 11.0 g/dL | N(11.3, 1.4)): 0.4152
P(X >= 20 cases | Binom(50, 0.35)): 0.2736
CLT mean of 2,000 means: 2.0023 (true=2.0)
Empirical SE: 0.3636 | Theoretical SE: 0.3651

Look closely at two details:

  • Survival function (sf): For discrete stats.binom, P(X >= 20) equals 1 - P(X <= 19) = stats.binom.sf(19, n=50, p=0.35) (note 19, not 20!).
  • CLT in action: Even though individual Exponential(scale=2.0) observations are severely skewed, the standard deviation of 2,000 sample means (0.3636) matches the theoretical standard error \(\sigma / \sqrt{30} = 2.0 / \sqrt{30} = 0.3651\) to three decimal places!

20.3.3 Proportion Estimation & 95% Wilson Score Confidence Interval

For a binomial proportion \(\hat{p} = k / n\) with critical value \(z\) (\(z = 1.96\) for 95% CI), the Wilson score interval centers and scales the interval so it never falls below \(0\), never exceeds \(1\), and remains accurate even at \(k = 0\):

\[d = 1 + \frac{z^2}{n}, \qquad c = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n}}{d}, \qquad m = \frac{z \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) + \frac{z^2}{4n}}{n}}}{d}\]

def wilson_ci_demo(successes, n, z=1.96):
    p = successes / n
    denom = 1 + (z ** 2) / n
    center = (p + (z ** 2) / (2 * n)) / denom
    margin = (
        z * math.sqrt((p * (1 - p) + (z ** 2) / (4 * n)) / n)
    ) / denom
    return (round(p, 4), round(max(0.0, center - margin), 4),
            round(min(1.0, center + margin), 4))

anemic_n = int(mch["maternal_anemia"].sum())
total_n = len(mch)
prop, lo, hi = wilson_ci_demo(anemic_n, total_n)
print(f"Maternal anemia: {anemic_n}/{total_n} = {prop:.4f} "
      f"(95% Wilson CI: [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}])")
print("Zero-event sample (0/20) Wilson CI:", wilson_ci_demo(0, 20))

Expected Output:

Maternal anemia: 179/500 = 0.3580 (95% Wilson CI: [0.3172, 0.4010])
Zero-event sample (0/20) Wilson CI: (0.0, 0.0, 0.1611)

Notice the 0/20 boundary check: a naive Wald interval \(\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\) collapses to [0.0, 0.0]—falsely claiming zero uncertainty! Wilson’s score interval returns [0.0, 0.1611], honestly showing that true prevalence could still be up to 16.1%.

20.3.4 Parametric Comparison: Welch’s \(t\)-Test & Pooled Cohen’s \(d\)

To quantify both statistical significance and standardized effect size between two continuous groups, pair Welch’s \(t\)-test (equal_var=False) with pooled Cohen’s \(d\):

\[s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_a - 1) s_a^2 + (n_b - 1) s_b^2}{n_a + n_b - 2}}, \qquad d = \frac{\bar{x}_a - \bar{x}_b}{s_p}\]

hb_urban = mch.loc[mch["residence"] == "Urban", "maternal_hb_g_dl"]
hb_rural = mch.loc[mch["residence"] == "Rural", "maternal_hb_g_dl"]
a = hb_urban.dropna().to_numpy()
b = hb_rural.dropna().to_numpy()

t_res = stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)
var_a, var_b = np.var(a, ddof=1), np.var(b, ddof=1)
pooled_sd = math.sqrt(
    ((len(a) - 1) * var_a + (len(b) - 1) * var_b) / (len(a) + len(b) - 2)
)
cohens_d = (a.mean() - b.mean()) / pooled_sd
print(f"Urban Hb mean: {a.mean():.3f} (n={len(a)}) | "
      f"Rural Hb mean: {b.mean():.3f} (n={len(b)})")
print(f"Welch t={t_res.statistic:.4f}, p={t_res.pvalue:.4f}, "
      f"Cohen's d={cohens_d:.4f}")

Expected Output:

Urban Hb mean: 11.598 (n=138) | Rural Hb mean: 11.406 (n=340)
Welch t=1.3273, p=0.1857, Cohen's d=0.1385

Urban mothers average 11.598 g/dL vs. 11.406 g/dL in rural areas—a very small effect (d = 0.1385) that is not statistically significant at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (p = 0.1857). Notice how .dropna() was essential because 22 mothers had missing hemoglobin readings!

20.3.5 Nonparametric Rank Tests: Mann-Whitney \(U\)

Antenatal care (anc_visits) is a discrete, right-skewed count variable with many ties. Instead of assuming normality, compare medians and run a two-sided Mann-Whitney \(U\) rank-sum test:

anc_urban = mch.loc[mch["residence"] == "Urban", "anc_visits"].dropna()
anc_rural = mch.loc[mch["residence"] == "Rural", "anc_visits"].dropna()

u_res = stats.mannwhitneyu(anc_urban, anc_rural, alternative="two-sided")
print(f"Median ANC visits — Urban: {anc_urban.median():.1f}, "
      f"Rural: {anc_rural.median():.1f}")
print(f"Mann-Whitney U={u_res.statistic:.2f}, "
      f"p={u_res.pvalue:.6f}, significant={bool(u_res.pvalue < 0.05)}")

Expected Output:

Median ANC visits — Urban: 5.0, Rural: 3.0
Mann-Whitney U=34177.50, p=0.000000, significant=True

Urban mothers receive a median of 5.0 ANC visits compared with 3.0 for rural mothers (p < 0.0001). Remember this striking rural–urban service gap—it will reappear as a classic confounder in our logistic regression!

20.3.6 Categorical Association: Pearson \(\chi^2\) & Fisher’s Exact Test

ct = pd.crosstab(mch["residence"], mch["maternal_anemia"])
a_cell = int(ct.loc["Rural", 1])
b_cell = int(ct.loc["Rural", 0])
c_cell = int(ct.loc["Urban", 1])
d_cell = int(ct.loc["Urban", 0])
table_2x2 = [[a_cell, b_cell], [c_cell, d_cell]]

chi2, p_chi2, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(
    table_2x2, correction=False
)
print("2x2 Table (Rural vs Urban x Anemia):", table_2x2)
print(f"Pearson chi2={chi2:.4f}, p={p_chi2:.4f}, "
      f"dof={dof}, expected_min={expected.min():.2f}")

# Small-cell subgroup where expected_min < 5 -> use Fisher's Exact
sparse_tbl = [[1, 14], [7, 8]]
_, _, _, exp_sparse = stats.chi2_contingency(sparse_tbl, correction=False)
fish_res = stats.fisher_exact(sparse_tbl)
print(f"Sparse expected_min={exp_sparse.min():.2f} -> "
      f"Fisher OR={fish_res.statistic:.3f}, p={fish_res.pvalue:.4f}")

Expected Output:

2x2 Table (Rural vs Urban x Anemia): [[133, 223], [46, 98]]
Pearson chi2=1.3081, p=0.2527, dof=1, expected_min=51.55
Sparse expected_min=4.00 -> Fisher OR=0.082, p=0.0352

For the full survey table, expected_min = 51.55 >= 5, so Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) (1.3081, p = 0.2527) is completely valid. In contrast, the sparse subgroup ([[1, 14], [7, 8]]) has expected_min = 4.00 < 5, triggering Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.0352).

20.3.7 Epidemiological Effect Measures: Risk Ratio & Woolf Odds Ratio 95% CI

In a standard \(2 \times 2\) epidemiological table (a = exposed cases, b = exposed non-cases, c = unexposed cases, d = unexposed non-cases):

\[\text{RR} = \frac{a / (a + b)}{c / (c + d)}, \qquad \text{OR} = \frac{ad}{bc}, \qquad \text{SE}(\ln \text{OR}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d}}\]

risk_rural = a_cell / (a_cell + b_cell)
risk_urban = c_cell / (c_cell + d_cell)
risk_ratio = risk_rural / risk_urban

odds_ratio = (a_cell * d_cell) / (b_cell * c_cell)
se_log_or = math.sqrt(1/a_cell + 1/b_cell + 1/c_cell + 1/d_cell)
ci_low = math.exp(math.log(odds_ratio) - 1.96 * se_log_or)
ci_high = math.exp(math.log(odds_ratio) + 1.96 * se_log_or)

print(f"Risk (Rural): {risk_rural:.4f} | Risk (Urban): {risk_urban:.4f}")
print(f"Risk Ratio (RR): {risk_ratio:.3f}")
print(f"Crude OR: {odds_ratio:.3f} "
      f"(95% Woolf CI: [{ci_low:.3f}, {ci_high:.3f}])")

Expected Output:

Risk (Rural): 0.3736 | Risk (Urban): 0.3194
Risk Ratio (RR): 1.170
Crude OR: 1.271 (95% Woolf CI: [0.843, 1.916])

Because the 95% CI [0.843, 1.916] spans the null value 1.0, the crude rural–urban anemia odds ratio is not statistically significant—completely consistent with Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) (p = 0.2527). Notice also that because anemia is common (~36%), the Odds Ratio (1.271) is noticeably further from 1.0 than the Risk Ratio (1.170).

20.3.8 Multiple Hypothesis Testing & Benjamini-Hochberg FDR

raw_p = [0.004, 0.032, 0.048, 0.210, 0.650]
bh_q = stats.false_discovery_control(raw_p, method="bh")
bonf_p = [min(1.0, p * len(raw_p)) for p in raw_p]

audit = pd.DataFrame({
    "raw_p": raw_p,
    "bh_q": np.round(bh_q, 4),
    "bonferroni_p": np.round(bonf_p, 4),
    "survives_fdr_05": bh_q < 0.05,
})
print(audit.to_string(index=False))

Expected Output:

 raw_p   bh_q  bonferroni_p  survives_fdr_05
 0.004 0.0200          0.02             True
 0.032 0.0800          0.16            False
 0.048 0.0800          0.24            False
 0.210 0.2625          1.00            False
 0.650 0.6500          1.00            False

Observe how three raw \(p\)-values sat below 0.05, yet after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction only 0.004 (q = 0.0200) remains significant! Notice also why both 0.032 and 0.048 yield bh_q = 0.0800: the BH step-up procedure enforces monotonicity from right to left (\(\min(0.032 \times 5 / 2, 0.048 \times 5 / 3) = \min(0.08, 0.08) = 0.08\)).

20.3.9 Multivariable Logistic Regression (statsmodels.api.Logit)

mch["is_urban"] = (mch["residence"] == "Urban").astype(int)
model_df = mch[["maternal_anemia", "anc_visits", "is_urban"]].dropna()
X = sm.add_constant(model_df[["anc_visits", "is_urban"]])
y = model_df["maternal_anemia"]

logit_fit = sm.Logit(y, X).fit(disp=False)
conf = logit_fit.conf_int()
aor_table = pd.DataFrame({
    "aor": np.exp(logit_fit.params),
    "ci_low": np.exp(conf[0]),
    "ci_high": np.exp(conf[1]),
    "p_value": logit_fit.pvalues,
}).round(4)
print(f"Complete cases fitted: n={len(model_df)}")
print(aor_table)

Expected Output:

Complete cases fitted: n=492
               aor  ci_low  ci_high  p_value
const       0.8293  0.5601   1.2277   0.3497
anc_visits  0.9006  0.8158   0.9941   0.0378
is_urban    0.9078  0.5877   1.4024   0.6630

Read this publication-ready Table 3 output carefully:

  • anc_visits (aOR = 0.9006, 95% CI 0.8158–0.9941, p = 0.0378): Controlling for urban/rural residence, each additional antenatal care visit is associated with a statistically significant 9.9% reduction in the odds of maternal anemia.
  • is_urban (aOR = 0.9078, 95% CI 0.5877–1.4024, p = 0.6630): Once ANC visit frequency is controlled for, urban vs. rural residence has no independent statistical effect—classic confounding uncovered by multivariable modeling!
  • Why sm.add_constant(X) and np.exp(...)? Unlike Scikit-Learn, Statsmodels does not add an intercept automatically, and its raw .params live on the log-odds (\(\beta\)) scale until exponentiated into Adjusted Odds Ratios (\(e^{\beta}\)).

20.3.10 Break It On Purpose! (Biostatistics Error Anatomy)

ImportantBreak It On Purpose #1 — ValueError (Negative Counts in Chi-Square Table) 
bad_table = [[-5, 20], [15, 30]]
stats.chi2_contingency(bad_table, correction=False)

Python’s Traceback:

ValueError: All values in `observed` must be nonnegative.

Diagnosis: Contingency tables represent event counts (\(\ge 0\)). Negative entries—often caused by passing differences, \(z\)-scores, or uncleaned sentinel codes like -99—immediately violate the \(\chi^2\) definition.

ImportantBreak It On Purpose #2 — ZeroDivisionError (Woolf SE With a Zero Cell)

Diagnosis: Woolf’s log-OR variance formula \(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d}\) blows up whenever any cell in the \(2 \times 2\) table equals 0.

def build_mch_evidence_summary(raw_df):
    """Run end-to-end prevalence, wealth-gradient & adjusted logit."""
    df = raw_df.copy()
    df["residence"] = df["residence"].astype(str).str.strip().str.title()
    df["anc_visits"] = df["anc_visits"].replace(999, np.nan)
    df["is_urban"] = (df["residence"] == "Urban").astype(int)

    # 1. Overall anemia prevalence + 95% Wilson CI
    prev = wilson_ci_demo(int(df["maternal_anemia"].sum()), len(df))

    # 2. Hemoglobin across 5 wealth quintiles (ANOVA + Kruskal-Wallis)
    groups = [
        grp["maternal_hb_g_dl"].dropna().to_numpy()
        for _, grp in df.groupby("wealth_quintile")
    ]
    f_stat, anova_p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
    h_stat, kruskal_p = stats.kruskal(*groups)

    # 3. Multivariable logistic regression (aOR table)
    m_df = df[["maternal_anemia", "anc_visits", "is_urban"]].dropna()
    X_m = sm.add_constant(m_df[["anc_visits", "is_urban"]])
    fit = sm.Logit(m_df["maternal_anemia"], X_m).fit(disp=False)
    ci = fit.conf_int()
    aor_df = pd.DataFrame({
        "aor": np.exp(fit.params),
        "ci_low": np.exp(ci[0]),
        "ci_high": np.exp(ci[1]),
        "p_value": fit.pvalues,
    }).round(4)
    wealth_tuple = (
        round(float(f_stat), 4), round(float(anova_p), 4),
        round(float(h_stat), 4), round(float(kruskal_p), 4),
    )
    return prev, wealth_tuple, aor_df

20.8.1 A. Concept & Execution Trace Test Key

  1. \(\text{SE}\) is cut in half! At \(n = 25\), \(\text{SE} = 2.0 / 5 = 0.40\); at \(n = 100\), \(\text{SE} = 2.0 / 10 = 0.20\). Quadrupling sample size halves the standard error (\(\sqrt{4} = 2\)).
  2. The Wald CI collapses to [0.0, 0.0], pretending zero uncertainty despite only 20 observations. Wilson’s score interval always stays inside [0, 1] and gives a realistic non-zero upper bound (0.1611) even at \(0/20\).
  3. By default equal_var=True, which runs Student’s equal-variance \(t\)-test. Pass equal_var=False for Welch’s \(t\)-test.
  4. Study B (d = 0.78, 1.10 g/dL) has far greater clinical magnitude! Huge sample sizes can make tiny, clinically trivial differences (0.05 g/dL) yield tiny \(p\)-values.
  5. Switch to Fisher’s exact test using scipy.stats.fisher_exact(table_2x2), because Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) asymptotic approximation degrades when expected_min < 5.
  6. \(\text{Risk}_{\text{exp}} = 40/100 = 0.40\), \(\text{Risk}_{\text{unexp}} = 20/100 = 0.20 \Rightarrow \mathbf{RR = 2.00}\). \(\mathbf{OR} = (40 \times 80)/(60 \times 20) = 3200 / 1200 \approx \mathbf{2.667}\). Because baseline risk (20%) is common, odds diverge from probabilities and \(\text{OR}\) exaggerates \(\text{RR}\).
  7. The CI is symmetric on the natural-log scale (\(\ln(\text{OR}) \pm 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}\)). Exponentiating a symmetric interval produces an asymmetric ratio interval on \((0, \infty)\).
  8. For \(m = 4\): \(q_1 = 0.01 \times 4 / 1 = \mathbf{0.04}\); \(q_2 = 0.04 \times 4 / 2 = \mathbf{0.08}\) (since \(q_3 = 0.20 \times 4 / 3 = 0.2667\) and \(q_4 = 0.80\), monotonicity is already satisfied).
  9. SciPy returns nan for both f_stat and anova_p (silent NaN propagation unless you drop NaNs first!).
  10. First, fit.params are log-odds (\(\beta\)), never odds ratios—you must exponentiate via np.exp(-0.1047) = 0.9006 (an Odds Ratio can never be negative!). Second, callers frequently forget sm.add_constant(X), forcing the regression through zero intercept.

import math
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# To-Do 20.1
def wilson_ci(successes, n, z=1.96):
    """Return (prop, low, high) 95% Wilson CI rounded to 4 decimals."""
    p = successes / n
    denom = 1.0 + (z ** 2) / n
    center = (p + (z ** 2) / (2.0 * n)) / denom
    margin = (
        z * math.sqrt((p * (1.0 - p) + (z ** 2) / (4.0 * n)) / n)
    ) / denom
    low = max(0.0, center - margin)
    high = min(1.0, center + margin)
    return (round(p, 4), round(low, 4), round(high, 4))

# To-Do 20.2
def compare_two_groups_welch(group_a, group_b):
    """Run Welch's t-test and compute pooled Cohen's d."""
    a = np.asarray(group_a, dtype=float)
    b = np.asarray(group_b, dtype=float)
    res = stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)
    n_a, n_b = len(a), len(b)
    var_a = float(np.var(a, ddof=1))
    var_b = float(np.var(b, ddof=1))
    pooled_sd = math.sqrt(
        ((n_a - 1) * var_a + (n_b - 1) * var_b) / (n_a + n_b - 2)
    )
    cohens_d = (float(np.mean(a)) - float(np.mean(b))) / pooled_sd
    return {
        "t_stat": round(float(res.statistic), 4),
        "p_value": round(float(res.pvalue), 4),
        "cohens_d": round(float(cohens_d), 4),
        "significant_05": bool(res.pvalue < 0.05),
    }

# To-Do 20.3
def nonparametric_mannwhitney(group_a, group_b):
    """Run a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test on two independent groups."""
    res = stats.mannwhitneyu(group_a, group_b, alternative="two-sided")
    return {
        "u_stat": round(float(res.statistic), 2),
        "p_value": round(float(res.pvalue), 4),
        "significant_05": bool(res.pvalue < 0.05),
    }

# To-Do 20.4
def chi2_contingency_audit(table_2x2):
    """Run Pearson chi-square (no Yates) and audit min expected count."""
    chi2, p_val, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(
        table_2x2, correction=False
    )
    return {
        "chi2": round(float(chi2), 4),
        "p_value": round(float(p_val), 4),
        "dof": int(dof),
        "expected_min": round(float(np.min(expected)), 2),
    }

# To-Do 20.5
def odds_ratio_with_ci(a, b, c, d):
    """Compute crude Odds Ratio and Woolf 95% CI rounded to 3 decimals."""
    or_val = (a * d) / (b * c)
    se = math.sqrt(1 / a + 1 / b + 1 / c + 1 / d)
    log_or = math.log(or_val)
    return {
        "or": round(or_val, 3),
        "ci_low": round(math.exp(log_or - 1.96 * se), 3),
        "ci_high": round(math.exp(log_or + 1.96 * se), 3),
    }

# To-Do 20.6
def benjamini_hochberg_adjust(p_values):
    """Return BH FDR-adjusted q-values as a 4-decimal Python list."""
    q_vals = stats.false_discovery_control(p_values, method="bh")
    return [round(float(q), 4) for q in q_vals]

import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm

# Exam Task 20.1 (30 pts)
def epidemiological_2x2_report(a, b, c, d):
    """Return full 2x2 risk, RR, OR, Woolf 95% CI, and chi2 p-value."""
    risk_exp = a / (a + b)
    risk_unexp = c / (c + d)
    rr = risk_exp / risk_unexp
    or_val = (a * d) / (b * c)
    se = math.sqrt(1 / a + 1 / b + 1 / c + 1 / d)
    log_or = math.log(or_val)
    _, p_val, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(
        [[a, b], [c, d]], correction=False
    )
    return {
        "risk_exposed": round(risk_exp, 4),
        "risk_unexposed": round(risk_unexp, 4),
        "risk_ratio": round(rr, 3),
        "odds_ratio": round(or_val, 3),
        "or_ci_low": round(math.exp(log_or - 1.96 * se), 3),
        "or_ci_high": round(math.exp(log_or + 1.96 * se), 3),
        "chi2_p_value": round(float(p_val), 4),
    }

# Exam Task 20.2 (35 pts)
def anova_and_kruskal_by_group(df, value_col, group_col):
    """Run NaN-safe One-Way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis across groups."""
    groups = [
        grp[value_col].dropna().to_numpy()
        for _, grp in df.groupby(group_col)
        if grp[value_col].notna().any()
    ]
    f_stat, anova_p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
    h_stat, kruskal_p = stats.kruskal(*groups)
    return {
        "n_groups": int(len(groups)),
        "f_stat": round(float(f_stat), 4),
        "anova_p": round(float(anova_p), 4),
        "h_stat": round(float(h_stat), 4),
        "kruskal_p": round(float(kruskal_p), 4),
    }

# Exam Task 20.3 (35 pts)
def fit_adjusted_anemia_logit(mch_df):
    """Clean MCH survey covariates and fit multivariable Logit aORs."""
    work = mch_df.copy()
    work["residence"] = (
        work["residence"].astype(str).str.strip().str.title()
    )
    work["anc_visits"] = work["anc_visits"].replace(999, np.nan)
    work["is_urban"] = (work["residence"] == "Urban").astype(int)
    model_df = work[
        ["maternal_anemia", "anc_visits", "is_urban"]

    X = sm.add_constant(model_df[["anc_visits", "is_urban"]])

            "aor": np.exp(fit.params),
            "ci_low": np.exp(conf[0]),
            "ci_high": np.exp(conf[1]),
            "p_value": fit.pvalues,
        },
        index=["const", "anc_visits", "is_urban"],

20.8.4 D. Debugging Post-Mortem: Top 3 Chapter 20 Failure Modes

  1. Forgetting equal_var=False or correction=False in SciPy: Leaving SciPy’s defaults silently switches Welch’s \(t\)-test to Student’s \(t\)-test and Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) to Yates’ corrected \(\chi^2\), shifting test statistics and \(p\)-values away from standard epidemiological reporting conventions.
  2. Leaving Survey Sentinels (999) or NaNs Uncleaned Before Modeling: Sentinel 999 in anc_visits inflates predictor variance by orders of magnitude and crushes the logistic slope toward zero, while uncleaned NaNs either propagate nan in f_oneway/kruskal or crash sm.Logit with MissingDataError.
  3. Reporting Raw Log-Odds Instead of Exponentiated aORs (or Omitting sm.add_constant): Statsmodels estimates linear log-odds \(\beta\) without an automatic intercept. Always wrap predictors with sm.add_constant(X) and apply np.exp(...) to both fit.params and fit.conf_int() before building your manuscript table.
